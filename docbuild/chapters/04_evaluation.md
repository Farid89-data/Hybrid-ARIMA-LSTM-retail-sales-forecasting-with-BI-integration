On M5, the recursive hybrid (hyb_z) was the most accurate model on every measure ({tbl:crossdataset}). Its MASE was 0.746, against 0.838 for ARIMA, the best single model, and 0.798 for the direct hybrid. The multi-step hybrid (0.847) was slightly less accurate than ARIMA. ETS (1.037) and the LSTM (1.081) were only moderately better than the seasonal naive method (1.196). The LSTM was also the least stable model, with a MASE between 1.032 and 1.116 across seeds; no hybrid had a standard deviation above 0.023.

On Rossmann, the multi-step hybrid (hyb_ms) was the most accurate (MASE 0.320), but the FFNN came close (0.328) ({tbl:crossdataset}). ETS (0.725) and the seasonal naive method (0.767), the only models without promotion information, had about twice the error. The hybrid with the lowest validation MASE, hyb_z on M5 (0.696) and hyb_ms on Rossmann (0.366), was also the best on the test period. The best residual design therefore differed between the datasets.

On M5, error grew with the horizon ({fig:m5horizon}). The recursive hybrid rose from 0.627 to 0.822 and led in every week. On Rossmann, the models with promotion information grew gently ({fig:rmhorizon}), the multi-step hybrid from 0.301 to 0.352. The seasonal naive method alternated between 0.947, 0.458, 1.116 and 0.570 in the four weeks, and ETS followed the same pattern. Both repeat the last observed week and ignore promotions; an interpretation is that the mostly fortnightly promotion cycle ({fig:rossseries}) gives weeks one and three the opposite promotion state to the repeated week.

{fig:m5example} shows one test window. All three models anticipated the drop on Thanksgiving Day, an event known in advance, but over-forecast the next day.

### Statistical significance and the hybrid's components (RQ2) {#sec:ressignificance}

The significance analysis is the one fixed in Chapter 3 before any result was available. The primary hybrid (the variant with the best validation MASE: the recursive hybrid on M5 and the multi-step hybrid on Rossmann) is compared with each of the seven other models, and the Holm correction is applied over these seven comparisons for each seed and loss ({tbl:m5dm} and {tbl:rmdm} in {app:D}). Every statement of significance in this report rests on that family. The matrices of all 28 pairs and the comparison of the other hybrid variants with their components are secondary, exploratory analyses ({sec:appsecondary} in {app:D}). The residual diagnostics that test the hybrid premise are in {app:D} ({tbl:m5resid}, {tbl:rmresid}).

A difference counts as robust only if it is significant in all five seeds. The p-values below are the median Holm-adjusted values over the five seeds.

On M5, the recursive hybrid was significantly more accurate than each of the seven other models, under both losses and in all five seeds (median adjusted p 0.0011 or less). Against ARIMA, its errors were significantly lower at seven of the ten stores and higher at none (seed 1, absolute loss, unadjusted per-store tests). Ten series is a small sample, so this result concerns these ten stores and may not carry over to other retailers. On Rossmann, the multi-step hybrid was significantly better than the seasonal naive method and ETS in all five seeds. Against the FFNN, the LSTM and the recursive hybrid, at most one seed was significant under either loss, so no hybrid was shown to be better than these models. Against ARIMA the multi-step hybrid was significant in only three of five seeds under both losses (median adjusted p 0.027 for absolute and 0.044 for squared loss), so it fails the five-seed rule despite a 10.8% lower MASE. This result is not robust: in the secondary 28-pair family the median adjusted p for squared loss is 0.064, above 0.05, against 0.044 in the primary family. No model was significantly better than the primary hybrid in any seed.

The secondary ablation ({tbl:m5ablation}, {tbl:rmablation} in {app:D}) compares every hybrid variant with ARIMA and the LSTM, with Holm adjustment over all 28 pairs. It is exploratory and supports no conclusion on its own. On M5, all three hybrids were significantly better than the LSTM, by 21.7% to 31.0% in MASE. The direct and recursive hybrids also beat ARIMA (4.8% and 11.0%; adjusted p 0.018 and 0.002), but the multi-step hybrid was 1.1% worse, not significantly. On Rossmann, only the recursive hybrid was significantly better than ARIMA (6.1%, p 0.012). No hybrid was significantly better than the LSTM or the FFNN, and the FFNN was significantly better than the LSTM. On M5 the LSTM was not significantly better than the seasonal naive method.

Two explanations fit these results, although neither was tested. First, the multi-step hybrid learns one 28-day error path per origin, so ten M5 stores give it few distinct targets; it was also the weakest hybrid on M5 validation (0.748). Second, on Rossmann the networks already receive the promotion indicator, which drives much of the variation, so ARIMA adds little they cannot learn: the FFNN, with no ARIMA input, was as accurate as the best hybrid.

The residual diagnostics test the premise of the hybrid ({tbl:m5resid}, {tbl:rmresid}). The Ljung-Box test at lags 7 and 14 found autocorrelation in the ARIMA residuals of none of the ten M5 stores and of four of the 20 Rossmann stores. The BDS test rejected independence for all ten M5 stores and 17 of the 20 Rossmann stores. ARIMA thus removed nearly all linear dependence, but some other dependence remained. This is consistent with the premise that the residuals hold something for a network to learn. It does not show that this structure is predictable: the BDS test also reacts to changing variance, and on Rossmann the networks alone did about as well.

With conformal intervals, test coverage ranged from 90.3% (LSTM) to 94.3% (recursive hybrid) on M5, and from 92.6% (ETS) to 96.3% (LSTM) on Rossmann ({tbl:m5intervals}, {tbl:rmintervals}). On M5 every model fell short of the nominal 95%; on Rossmann, ARIMA, the FFNN and the LSTM exceeded it.

Conformal calibration did not always improve on the models' own intervals. ARIMA's Gaussian intervals covered only 90.8% on M5, but they were narrower and had a better interval score than the conformal ones (6.86 against 7.26). On Rossmann they covered 96.7% with the lowest interval score of all (2.70). The conformal method's value is that every model, including the networks, receives an interval built in the same way.

Coverage varied strongly with the horizon ({fig:m5covh}, {fig:rmcovh} in {app:D}). For most models it was highest in the first week and lowest in the third. On Rossmann, third-week coverage was 89.2% for the multi-step hybrid, 89.9% for ARIMA and 74.7% for ETS. Single horizons fell further, for example to 74.7% for the multi-step hybrid at horizon 22 on Rossmann. A planner should therefore not read the band as a 95% guarantee for any particular day. {sec:limitations} explains why the guarantee is only approximate.

ARIMA dominated the computational cost. Its order search and fitting took 84.3 minutes for M5 and 92.0 minutes for Rossmann, more than any network including tuning. Every hybrid inherits this cost, so the hybrids were the most expensive models ({tbl:cost}). The recursive hybrid's figures exclude the direct hybrid's tuning search, which it reused (21.5 and 16.6 minutes).

The accuracy-cost plots ({fig:m5cost}, {fig:rmcost}) show two different trade-offs. On M5, the recursive hybrid reduced MASE by 11.0% relative to ARIMA for 96.3 instead of 84.3 minutes, plus the reused search. On Rossmann, the FFNN reached a MASE of 0.328, against 0.320 for the best hybrid, for about a tenth of the cost, and the two were not significantly different.

All final experiments took 25,649 seconds (7.1 hours), or about 0.11 kWh. Energy was estimated as 15 W multiplied by runtime, so it is a proxy for runtime, not a separate measure. The runtimes are indicative: most stages ran in parallel lanes that shared the processor, and the Rossmann ARIMA times include contention from a duplicate run. Large differences, such as ARIMA against the FFNN, are robust to this; small ones are not.

### Decision support (RQ4) {#sec:resdashboard}

The data side of the dashboard is complete. The export script wrote the star schema of {fig:schema}: three dimension tables (1,941 dates, 30 series and eight models) and three fact tables (38,250 actuals, 62,720 forecast rows and 240 metric rows). It also wrote acceptance values for every dataset and model, including coverage, store-average MASE and the stock-out and overstock risk days. The measures and build instructions are in {app:F}, and the functional test plan is in {app:E}. The export uses seed 1 for the networks, so its network MASE values differ slightly from the five-seed means (for example, 0.881 against 0.864 for the M5 FFNN).

The rest is not complete. The Power BI file has not been assembled, so the functional tests in {app:E} and the accessibility checks in {tbl:a11y} have not been run; they are to be run by the author. The user study has not been conducted. RQ4 is therefore answered by design, not by evidence of use.

### Answers to the research questions {#sec:rqanswers}

**RQ1.** A hybrid was the most accurate model on both datasets, but not the same one. On M5, the recursive hybrid reduced MASE by 11.0% relative to ARIMA. The multi-step hybrid was 1.1% worse than ARIMA on M5. On Rossmann, the multi-step hybrid had a 10.8% lower MASE than ARIMA, but this gain is not robust (see RQ2), and it improved only marginally on the FFNN (0.320 against 0.328).

**RQ2.** On M5, the hybrid's advantage was significant against all seven alternatives, under both losses and in all five seeds. On Rossmann, it was robust only against the seasonal naive method and ETS; against ARIMA it held in three of five seeds (squared loss: median adjusted p 0.044 in the primary family, 0.064 in the secondary 28-pair family), so it is not robust, and against the networks and the other hybrids in at most one seed. Ten M5 series is a small sample.

**RQ3.** Conformal coverage ranged from 90.3% to 96.3%. It was below 95% for all eight models on M5 and for four of eight on Rossmann (seasonal naive, ETS, multi-step and recursive hybrids), and it varied by horizon. ARIMA dominated the cost, which made the hybrids the most expensive models. On Rossmann, the FFNN gave nearly the same accuracy for about a tenth of the cost.

**RQ4.** The data model, measures, acceptance values and test plan show how the forecasts can be integrated into Power BI. Whether the dashboard meets its functional, accessibility and usability requirements has not yet been tested.

## Evaluation of the Process {#sec:process}

### Methodology

Combining CRISP-DM with short increments worked well for the analytical core of the project. Each increment ended with an artefact that could be tested before the next one depended on it. This mattered most at the boundary between the statistical and neural stages. The hybrids need ARIMA residuals and forecast paths that are aligned day by day with the calendar and free of look-ahead. Because these artefacts were produced and tested in their own increment, an alignment error could be detected by a failing test rather than by an implausible result weeks later. The decisions log was equally useful. It recorded, for example, why closures are passed to the state-space models as missing values and why the hybrids were compared in three variants, so that each choice could be justified against its alternatives rather than reconstructed afterwards.

The weaknesses of CRISP-DM noted in {sec:bilit} also appeared. The process model says little about how long modelling and evaluation take on limited hardware, and the plan in the proposal assumed that every model could be tuned and retrained within a week. In practice, computation was the binding constraint. The response, a small tuning budget and checkpointed background runs, was sound but should have been planned from the start.

### What went well

- **Data integrity first.** Checking every raw file before modelling, and recording checksums, meant that every later result can be traced to identical inputs.
- **Leakage prevention by design.** The rolling-origin plan, scalers fitted on training data only and the perturbation tests turned the main methodological risk of the reviewed studies ({sec:hybridlit}) into something that is tested automatically.
- **Numbers generated, not typed.** Every number in the result tables is produced by the evaluation scripts from saved forecasts, which removes transcription errors and makes the report reproducible.
- **Verified literature.** Each source was checked against its DOI record and read in full. A second check of the comparison table in {sec:hybridlit} found and corrected one wrong cell, which shows why this step was necessary.

### What went less well

- **Literature access.** Automated searching of Google Scholar was not possible, and several large publishers refused automated access to full texts. The review therefore rests on 47 articles from a narrower set of publishers than intended, below the 60 to 80 sources that had been planned.
- **Toolchain deviations.** The planned R implementation and cross-check were not carried out, and Python 3.13 replaced Python 3.11.
- **Interrupted computation.** A background run was stopped by the operating environment and its progress was lost, which led to the checkpointing described in {sec:issues}.
- **User evaluation.** Ethical approval for the user study was granted, but the study could not be conducted within the time available, and the dashboard file was not assembled in Power BI Desktop. Objective 5 is therefore only partly met.

### What would be done differently

The user study would be scheduled in parallel with model development rather than after it, because it depends on recruitment and on the dashboard rather than on the final model. Compute would be planned explicitly, with an estimate of training time per model before the tuning budget is fixed, and with access to a graphics processor or cloud resources. Finally, the literature search would start with the library's databases, which provide full-text access, rather than relying on open web access.

## Limitations {#sec:limitations}

The findings are bounded by the following limitations:

- **Scope of the data.** Only store-level daily series were modelled: ten Walmart stores and a sample of 20 Rossmann stores. Results may not transfer to item-level forecasting, where intermittent demand dominates, or to other retail formats and markets. The Rossmann sample was drawn from the 850 of 1,115 stores that met the regular-trading conditions, and store type "b" was excluded, so it represents regular-trading stores only.
- **Information set.** Prices were not used as covariates, because they are recorded per item and do not aggregate meaningfully to store level. Weather, competitor actions and local events were not available.
- **Fixed parameters between origins.** Model parameters were estimated once before the test period and kept fixed at later origins. Periodic re-estimation might improve all models, and its effect on the ranking was not tested.
- **One test period per dataset.** The test periods cover 392 days for M5 and 196 days for Rossmann. A different period, for example one containing a structural break such as the COVID-19 pandemic, could change the conclusions.
- **Tuning budget.** Fifteen tuning trials per network is a small budget. Larger searches might improve the networks, although @tetko2024 suggests that the gains from extensive tuning are often small.
- **Interval guarantees.** Split conformal intervals assume that test errors are exchangeable with the calibration errors. Here this holds only approximately. The calibration errors come from the tuning-stage models, for the networks the best trial with seed 1, while the test forecasts come from re-estimated models and five seeds. Each horizon's quantile rests on at most 50 errors for M5 (five validation windows and 10 stores) and 100 for Rossmann, fewer where days were closed. At rank \(\lceil (n+1)\,0.95 \rceil\), this is the second-largest of 50 errors, so the quantile is itself uncertain. Pooling errors across stores assumes that scaled errors behave alike in every store. Under distribution shift, coverage can fall short by an amount that grows with the shift [@schlembach2025]. For the networks, the calibration windows also selected the hyperparameters, which may make their intervals slightly too narrow.
- **Significance tests.** The DM test was applied to losses pooled over horizons 1 to 28 from non-overlapping windows. The HLN correction is an approximation for this design. With seven Rossmann windows the test has little power, so a non-significant difference is not evidence that two models are equally accurate.
- **Residuals and parameters.** The hybrids use causally filtered residuals, but the ARIMA parameters behind them were estimated on the same training period on which the networks learn. The training residuals may therefore be slightly smaller than those after the origin.
- **Runtime and energy.** Runtimes come from processes that shared the processor, and energy is estimated from runtime ({sec:rescost}). Both suit comparisons between models with large differences, not absolute figures.
- **No user evidence.** Without the user study, there is no evidence yet about whether the dashboard helps planners make better decisions.

## Ethical, Social and Sustainability Considerations {#sec:ethics}

The project used public, anonymised retail data at store level. No personal data were processed, and the Kaggle files were used for research only and are not redistributed with the code. The user study involves human participants. It has ethical approval and will follow the consent and data-handling commitments of the proposal ({app:G}). No participant data have been collected so far.

The social effects of forecasting depend on how forecasts are used. Forecasts inform orders and staff rosters, so systematic errors can lead to waste or to shortages, and staff may resist algorithmic recommendations [@winkler2023]. Organisations also have to balance human and algorithmic agency in such decisions [@wang2026]. The dashboard was therefore designed to support rather than replace planners. It shows uncertainty and bias alongside the forecast, explains each planning indicator in words and keeps the user in control of the final decision [@cheong2024]. Promotions illustrate the wider effect of retail decisions: they raise sales, but they can also increase household food waste [@gravert2025]. A forecasting system that makes promotions more profitable without considering waste would serve the retailer but not necessarily society.

Sustainability was treated as part of the evaluation rather than as an afterthought. Training and tuning time were measured for every model and converted into estimated energy, so that any accuracy gain of the networks can be weighed against its computational cost [@tabbakh2024; @rafat2023]. The same reasoning motivated the small tuning budget and the decision to keep model parameters fixed between forecast origins [@zanotti2026].

Finally, the project has a global dimension. The data come from retailers in the United States and Germany. Calendar effects, promotion practices and the availability of data differ between countries, so a framework that relies on known-in-advance calendar and promotion information would need local calendars and local validation before it is used elsewhere. The approach is, however, open and inexpensive: it runs on a laptop without a graphics processor, which makes it accessible to smaller retailers and to organisations with limited computing resources.
